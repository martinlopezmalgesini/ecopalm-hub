## Deal stages and their gates

### Stage 0: Prepare and verify the parties
*Goal:* Know exactly who you are dealing with before sharing anything.

**Gate checklist:**
- Who signs the sales contract, issues the invoice and receives the LC proceeds?
- Company registry, directors and beneficial owners checked
- Sanctions and adverse-media screening done
- ISCC and licence numbers verified in public databases
- Volume plausible for the stated capacity
- Chain mapped: no more than two undocumented layers

**Common traps:**
- Blind chains that refuse video calls
- Mandates that stop before the owner
- Hidden related companies

### Stage 1: Demand, offer and protection
*Goal:* Turn a lead into a protected, priced opportunity.

**Gate checklist:**
- Buyer demand has a price basis: benchmark, premium or discount, basis and pricing period
- Seller offer names the product, spec, port, window and validity
- Proof of product is verifiable, not just PDFs
- Commission agreement and NCNDA signed before identities are shared
- No fees requested before a verified contract

**Common traps:**
- ICPO without a price basis
- Soft proof of product
- Fee-for-sample demands

### Stage 2: Negotiation and contract
*Goal:* Write every duty down before anything moves.

**Gate checklist:**
- Price formula and benchmark defined
- Incoterm named with its edition
- Inspector, sampling and final-and-binding rule agreed
- Sustainability warranties and documents included
- Law, arbitration seat and change-in-law clause agreed
- Brokerage arrangement stays in a separate agreement

**Common traps:**
- Mismatch between contract and LC terms
- Silent contracts on sustainability
- Naming the broker's fee where it should not appear

### Stage 3: Payment security
*Goal:* Make sure the money is guaranteed before the oil leaves.

**Gate checklist:**
- LC text compared line by line with the contract
- Issuing bank and confirmation decided
- LC opened before the loading window
- Document list achievable by the seller
- Banking charges allocated
- Commission payment route agreed in writing

**Common traps:**
- PDF letters of credit
- Instrument monetization offers
- Escrow proposed for a bulk cargo

### Stage 4: Logistics, inspection and loading
*Goal:* Ship the right cargo on a verified vessel with independent measurement.

**Gate checklist:**
- Vessel IMO, ownership and sanctions checked
- Tank inspection and cleanliness confirmed
- Independent inspector appointed and briefed
- Sealed samples taken and retained
- Quantity and quality certificates consistent

**Common traps:**
- Ghost vessels
- Unknown or non-independent inspectors
- Off-spec cargo loaded under pressure

### Stage 5: Transit and documents
*Goal:* Move the paper as carefully as the oil.

**Gate checklist:**
- Bill of lading clean and shipped on board
- Documents match the LC line by line
- Sustainability proof and Union Database records in order
- Export and import customs papers consistent
- Plan for documents arriving after the ship

**Common traps:**
- Switch bills hiding origin
- Improvised letters of indemnity
- Small spelling discrepancies that block payment

### Stage 6: Discharge, claims, payment and commission
*Goal:* Close the deal: settle claims, get paid, get your fee.

**Gate checklist:**
- Discharge inspection done
- Claims noticed within contract time limits
- Documents presented and bank payment received
- Commission paid as agreed
- Records kept for audits

**Common traps:**
- Missing a claim deadline
- Commission left to goodwill
- Records that do not match certificates


## Payment methods and instruments

### Payment-method spectrum: who carries the risk
`pay-spectrum` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: foundation | confidence: high

**In plain words:** Every payment method decides who has to trust whom: the seller, the buyer or a bank.

**In detail:** Ranked from most seller-friendly to most buyer-friendly: cash in advance, confirmed irrevocable LC, unconfirmed irrevocable LC, documentary collection D/P, D/A, open account (optionally insured). Real deals blend methods, e.g. a down payment plus an LC. The side with more bargaining power usually imposes the method, so the method itself shows who needs the deal more.

**Options and variants:**
- Cash in advance (T/T): Seller safest, buyer most exposed
- Confirmed irrevocable LC: Two banks promise to pay
- Unconfirmed irrevocable LC: One bank promises to pay
- Documentary collection D/P: Banks release documents against payment, no guarantee
- Documentary collection D/A: Documents released against a promise to pay later
- Open account: Seller finances the buyer; needs insurance or standby

**For a broker:** Your commission is only as safe as the payment method. Ask for it early.

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview; T/T in advance (prepayment, incl. split payment); Open account

### T/T in advance (prepayment, incl. split payment)
`pay-tt-advance` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: foundation | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The buyer wires money before the goods ship.

**In detail:** Telegraphic transfer (SWIFT MT103) paid before shipment, in full or as a down payment with the balance against documents. Best for the seller, worst for the buyer. Legitimate between parties with a proven record, small lots or scarce supply. With an unverified seller it is the classic advance-fee pattern. Safer versions: refundable advance backed by an advance payment guarantee, escrow with a regulated agent, or payment against an independent inspector's certificate.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Total loss if the seller does not exist
- Often disguised as 'registration', 'inspection' or 'bond' fees

**For a broker:** Never steer a buyer into prepaying an unverified seller.

**Related:** Advance payment guarantee (APG); Escrow; Advance-fee schemes

**Verification note:** Original document called this 'never acceptable'; it is a legitimate but high-risk method.

### Cash against documents (CAD)
`pay-cad` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** Payment is due when the seller presents the shipping documents.

**In detail:** A contract term, not a formal bank procedure: the buyer pays on presentation of the agreed documents (original B/L, certificates), usually via its bank. The seller keeps control of the cargo while originals are held. If the buyer refuses to pay, cargo can sit in port and generate demurrage and storage costs. Do not confuse with documentary collection D/P, which follows ICC URC 522.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Stranded cargo if buyer refuses
- No bank payment undertaking

**Related:** Documentary collection - D/P (documents against payment)

### Documentary collection - D/P (documents against payment)
`pay-dp` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** Banks hand the shipping documents to the buyer only when the buyer pays.

**In detail:** The seller ships and sends documents to its bank (remitting bank), which forwards them to the buyer's bank (collecting/presenting bank). The buyer receives the documents, and with them the cargo, only after paying. Banks handle documents only and give no payment guarantee, so the seller carries buyer risk and stranded-cargo risk.

**Governing rules:** ICC URC 522

**Risks and red flags:**
- No guarantee of payment
- Cargo may be refused at destination

**Related:** Cash against documents (CAD); Documentary collection - D/A (documents against acceptance)

### Documentary collection - D/A (documents against acceptance)
`pay-da` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The buyer gets the documents by signing a promise to pay later.

**In detail:** The seller draws a time draft (bill of exchange) payable e.g. 30, 60 or 90 days after sight or B/L date. The buyer accepts it to receive the documents and the cargo, and pays at maturity. A bank can add an 'aval' (guarantee) to the draft. It is an accepted draft, not a simple promissory note. Highest non-payment risk among collection methods because the cargo is already released.

**Governing rules:** ICC URC 522; Local bills-of-exchange law

**Risks and red flags:**
- Cargo released before payment

**Related:** Documentary collection - D/P (documents against payment)

### Open account
`pay-open-account` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** Goods ship first; the buyer pays later by wire.

**In detail:** Standard between established partners. The seller extends unsecured credit (often 30-90 days) and carries all buyer risk. Mitigations: trade credit insurance, standby L/C or bank guarantee, factoring or forfaiting, parent guarantees. Not suitable for new counterparties.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Unsecured exposure for the full invoice

**Related:** Trade credit insurance; Standby letter of credit (SBLC); Trade-finance toolbox

**Verification note:** Original called it 'commercial suicide'; accurate only without protection.

### Trade credit insurance
`pay-credit-insurance` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** Insurance that pays the seller if the buyer does not pay.

**In detail:** Private insurers or export credit agencies cover commercial and political non-payment risk, normally for less than 100% of the invoice and within buyer credit limits. Disputes about quality or performance are generally excluded.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Policy limits and exclusions
- Insurer may withdraw cover on a weakening buyer

**Related:** Open account

### Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview
`pay-lc` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: foundation | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A bank's promise to pay the seller if the seller presents exactly the documents the credit requires.

**In detail:** Governed by ICC UCP 600. Parties: applicant (buyer), issuing bank, advising bank, beneficiary (seller), optional confirming bank, nominated bank. Flow: sale contract, buyer applies, issuing bank issues (SWIFT MT700), advising bank advises, seller ships and presents documents, banks examine (up to 5 banking days, art. 14), then pay, accept or negotiate. Principles: independent of the sale contract (art. 4); banks deal in documents, not goods (art. 5); strict compliance; credits are irrevocable (art. 3); presentation normally within 21 calendar days after shipment and before expiry (art. 14(c)).

**Options and variants:**
- At sight: Paid after compliant presentation
- Deferred payment / usance: Paid at a later date
- Acceptance credit: Bank accepts a time draft
- Negotiation credit: Nominated bank buys the documents; restricted or freely negotiable
- Confirmed / silent confirmation: Second bank adds its promise
- Transferable: Pass to supplier(s)
- Back-to-back: Second credit backed by the first
- Revolving: Reinstates for repeated shipments
- Red / green clause: Pre-shipment advance
- Standby (SBLC): Pays only on default

**Governing rules:** ICC UCP 600; ISBP 745

**Risks and red flags:**
- Any discrepancy can block payment even if the oil is perfect
- Issuing-bank credit risk unless confirmed

**For a broker:** Check LC terms against the SPA before loading; your commission usually depends on the LC paying.

**Related:** UCP 600: key articles to know; Common LC discrepancies and what to do; LC costs and who pays

### LC at sight
`pay-lc-sight` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The bank pays once it has checked that the documents comply.

**In detail:** Payment follows a compliant presentation. UCP 600 gives the bank up to five banking days after presentation to examine, so 'at sight' is not same-day. Payment may come from the issuing, nominated or confirming bank. On discrepancies the bank may refuse or ask the applicant for a waiver.

**Governing rules:** UCP 600 art. 14

**Risks and red flags:**
- Discrepancy stops payment

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview; Common LC discrepancies and what to do

**Verification note:** Original said 'about 3-5 days after B/L'; the rule is a maximum of 5 banking days to examine.

### Deferred-payment and acceptance LC (usance)
`pay-lc-deferred` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The bank pays at a later date, e.g. 60 days after the B/L date.

**In detail:** Maturity is counted from the date stated in the credit: B/L date, presentation, acceptance or sight. Two forms: deferred payment undertaking (no draft) and acceptance credit (bank accepts a draft, which can be discounted for early cash). Gives the buyer credit time; the seller can discount at a cost. Sellers usually want such credits confirmed.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Discounting cost erodes margin
- Weak issuing bank

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview; Confirmed LC (incl. silent confirmation); Trade-finance toolbox

### Confirmed LC (incl. silent confirmation)
`pay-lc-confirmed` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A second bank adds its own promise to pay.

**In detail:** A confirming bank, usually in the seller's country, takes on the undertaking in addition to the issuing bank (UCP 600 art. 8), removing issuing-bank and country risk for the seller. The fee is negotiated and often borne by the beneficiary. 'Silent confirmation' means the seller arranges confirmation privately with a bank, without the issuing bank's involvement.

**Governing rules:** UCP 600 art. 8

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview

### Transferable LC
`pay-lc-transferable` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The seller, often a trader, can pass all or part of the credit to its supplier.

**In detail:** Must be expressly marked 'transferable' (UCP 600 art. 38). The first beneficiary can transfer to one or more second beneficiaries; a second beneficiary cannot request a further transfer. The trader may substitute its own invoice (and draft), keeping its margin and hiding supplier or buyer identity. The transferred credit may reduce amount, unit price, shipment period and expiry, and may increase insurance cover. Needs a bank willing to act as transferring bank.

**Governing rules:** UCP 600 art. 38

**Risks and red flags:**
- Second beneficiary relies on a credit issued for someone else
- Can be combined with switch bills to hide origin

**For a broker:** Typical for traders without production; brokers rarely benefit directly.

**Related:** Switch bill of lading; Back-to-back LC

### Back-to-back LC
`pay-lc-b2b` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: advanced | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** Two separate credits: the second one is backed by the first.

**In detail:** The trader's buyer opens a master LC in the trader's favour. The trader uses it as security to have its own bank open a second LC for its supplier. The two credits are independent, so mismatches between them are a real risk. Depends on the trader's bank appetite and credit line; costs add up.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Mismatched terms between the two credits
- Bank may refuse

**Related:** Transferable LC

### Assignment of LC proceeds
`pay-lc-assignment` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: advanced | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The seller instructs that part of the money from the LC goes to someone else.

**In detail:** UCP 600 art. 39: a credit that is not transferable still lets the beneficiary assign proceeds under applicable law. It covers only the money, not the right to perform under the credit. Used to pay suppliers or other parties from proceeds. The bank must acknowledge it and is not obliged to act without agreeing.

**Governing rules:** UCP 600 art. 39

**For a broker:** One way a commission payout can be tied to LC proceeds, if the bank agrees.

**Related:** How a broker actually gets paid

### Revolving LC
`pay-lc-revolving` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: advanced | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** A credit that renews automatically for repeated shipments.

**In detail:** Reinstates its amount after each drawing (by value or by time; cumulative or non-cumulative). Fits term supply such as monthly cargoes: one facility instead of opening a new LC each month. Requires careful drafting of reinstatement conditions.

**For a broker:** Relevant to a monthly programme such as 5,000 t/month.

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview; SPA variants

### Red-clause and green-clause LC
`pay-lc-red-green` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: advanced | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** A credit that lets the seller draw an advance before shipping.

**In detail:** Red clause: advance against a simple receipt or undertaking to ship. Green clause: also covers storage of goods before shipment. Used for pre-export finance. Heavy risk for the applicant if goods never ship.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Applicant bears advance risk

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview; Advance payment guarantee (APG)

### Standby letter of credit (SBLC)
`pay-sblc` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A bank guarantee that pays only if the buyer fails to pay.

**In detail:** Governed by ISP98 or, to the extent applicable, UCP 600 or URDG 758. Normally issued by SWIFT MT760. Drawn by presenting a statement of default. Backs open-account trades, financial obligations or performance. A commercial LC is the primary payment route; a standby is the fallback. Unlike a documentary LC, it is not normally the way the seller gets paid.

**Governing rules:** ISP98; UCP 600; URDG 758

**Related:** Demand guarantee (URDG 758); Instrument 'monetization' and leasing schemes

**Verification note:** Original said 'only if open account is used'; standbys are also used as financial or performance guarantees.

### Demand guarantee (URDG 758)
`pay-demand-guarantee` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A bank promise to pay on first written demand, independent of the contract.

**In detail:** Governed by ICC URDG 758. Types: performance guarantee, advance payment guarantee, bid/tender bond, payment guarantee, retention or warranty guarantee. Payment is triggered by a compliant demand, not by proof of default in the contract.

**Options and variants:**
- Performance guarantee: Compensates non-delivery
- Advance payment guarantee: Refund of a prepayment
- Bid / tender bond: Backs a tender offer
- Payment guarantee: Backs the buyer's payment
- Retention / warranty: Covers post-delivery obligations

**Governing rules:** ICC URDG 758

**Related:** Performance bond (PB); Advance payment guarantee (APG)

### Performance bond (PB)
`pay-performance-bond` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A guarantee from the seller's bank that pays the buyer if the seller does not deliver.

**In detail:** Common in tenders and large supply contracts. The percentage and wording are negotiated; there is no standard percentage. In unsolicited broker-chain deals, a performance bond demanded early, or a fee demanded to obtain one from a 'provider', is a classic advance-fee pattern.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Fee-for-bond requests from intermediaries

**Related:** Demand guarantee (URDG 758); Advance-fee schemes

**Verification note:** Original quoted 2%-3%; that is not a market standard.

### Advance payment guarantee (APG)
`pay-apg` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The seller's bank guarantees to refund any advance if the seller does not deliver.

**In detail:** Makes a prepayment safer for the buyer. Issued under URDG 758 or similar. Should be issued by a bank the buyer can verify directly, and should reduce as delivery progresses.

**Governing rules:** ICC URDG 758

**Related:** T/T in advance (prepayment, incl. split payment); Demand guarantee (URDG 758)

### Escrow
`pay-escrow` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** A neutral party holds the money until the agreed conditions are met.

**In detail:** A regulated bank or law firm client account holds funds and releases them against agreed evidence such as an inspector's certificate or a B/L. For bulk cargoes it is rarely used: millions of dollars sit locked while release depends on a third party's confirmation, and a ship waiting for that release runs up demurrage that can reach tens of thousands of dollars a day. The standard instrument for bulk is the letter of credit, confirmed if needed, or a standby-backed arrangement. Escrow fits small parcels such as ISO tank or container lots, deposits, or holding a performance deposit. The agent must be independently verifiable, and conflicts of interest arise if one side chooses and pays the agent.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Not a bulk-cargo standard: locked funds and demurrage risk
- Fake 'escrow' websites
- Agent not regulated or not independent

**For a broker:** If a buyer or seller proposes escrow for a full cargo, ask why not a letter of credit.

**Related:** Fake escrow services; Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview; Standby letter of credit (SBLC)

**Verification note:** Earlier version did not stress that escrow is not a bulk-cargo standard.

### Trade-finance toolbox
`pay-trade-finance` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: advanced | confidence: high

**In plain words:** Ways to get cash earlier or carry the working capital of a deal.

**In detail:** These are financing, not payment-security, instruments. They sit on top of an LC or receivable.

**Options and variants:**
- LC discounting: Bank pays now against a deferred LC, deducts interest
- Forfaiting: Non-recourse purchase of deferred payment obligations
- Factoring / receivables finance: Advance against invoices
- Pre-export finance: Funds production before shipment
- Inventory / warehouse-receipt finance: Loans against stored goods
- Supply-chain finance: Bank pays suppliers early on buyer approval
- Structured commodity finance: Facility secured by goods and contracts

**Related:** Deferred-payment and acceptance LC (usance)

### Proof of funds (PoF)
`pay-pof` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 1 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A document showing that the buyer has the money.

**In detail:** Bank statement, audited accounts or a credit line letter. As a PDF it is easy to forge, so it is weak evidence. Verify by having your bank or the seller's bank contact the issuing bank through independently sourced contacts. A verifiable credit line or an LC from a first-class bank is stronger.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Forged or recycled statements
- Funds that are not available for the deal

**Related:** Bank comfort letter (BCL) and 'ready, willing and able' (RWA) letter; MT799 free-format messages

### Bank comfort letter (BCL) and 'ready, willing and able' (RWA) letter
`pay-bcl-rwa` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 1 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A bank letter saying a client is able to do the deal.

**In detail:** Non-binding. Used for credibility, not payment. If a buyer's bank will not issue one, the substitute is audited financials, a credit report or a verifiable credit line, not a public corporate profile.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Letters from the buyer's own finance officer are not bank confirmations

**Related:** Proof of funds (PoF)

**Verification note:** Original suggested substituting a public corporate profile; that is not evidence of payment capacity.

### How a broker actually gets paid
`pay-broker-commission` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 1, 3, 6 | level: foundation | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** Your fee comes from a written agreement and a bank wire, not from the sale contract itself.

**In detail:** Typical routes: (1) commission agreement with the seller or buyer, paid by wire after payment under the SPA; (2) payment instruction or assignment of LC proceeds, if the bank agrees; (3) a separate agreement with a payout trigger such as 'seller has received payment'; (4) roll-over clause for later shipments; (5) trading margin if you take title (then you are a trader, not a broker). A reference to the broker's fee inside the SPA does not make the bank pay it automatically.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Commission depends on the payer's honesty if there is no instruction to a bank
- Chains where each link expects a share

**For a broker:** Define payer, trigger, currency, wire route, duration and roll-overs in writing.

**Related:** Commission / fee agreement (direct); IMFPA (irrevocable master fee protection agreement); Assignment of LC proceeds

### Currency and price hedging
`pay-fx-hedging` | sector: Market and pricing | stages: 3 | level: advanced | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** Protecting the deal from exchange-rate and price swings.

**In detail:** Traders who hold price exposure may hedge with swaps or futures on correlated products; invoices in a currency other than the payer's own create FX risk that forwards can cover. A pure broker with no title has little direct exposure, but the buyer and seller price formulas still move the deal.

**Related:** Price formulas

### LC costs and who pays
`pay-lc-costs` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** Banks charge fees at every step; the contract says who pays.

**In detail:** Typical charges: issuance, advising, confirmation, amendment, discrepancy, negotiation or discounting. Issuance cost depends on the applicant's credit and any cash margin required. Common wording puts charges outside the issuing bank's country on the beneficiary. For the buyer, an LC also uses up credit lines or ties up cash.

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview


## Banking rules and messages

### The ICC and banking rulebook
`bank-rulebook` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** The set of rules banks and traders use so documents and payments work the same way everywhere.

**In detail:** Most trade-finance and shipping rules come from the ICC. They apply only when incorporated by reference in the contract or instrument. Market-custom papers such as NCNDA, IMFPA, ICPO, SCO and FCO are not ICC instruments.

**Options and variants:**
- UCP 600: Documentary credits
- ISBP 745: How banks examine documents under UCP 600
- eUCP 2.1: Electronic presentation under UCP
- URC 522: Documentary collections
- URDG 758: Demand guarantees
- ISP98: Standby letters of credit
- URR 725: Bank-to-bank reimbursement
- URF 800: Forfaiting
- Incoterms 2020: Delivery terms

**Related:** UCP 600: key articles to know

**Verification note:** Check current edition numbers (e.g. eUCP) on the ICC site.

### UCP 600: key articles to know
`bank-ucp600-articles` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: advanced | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The few articles that decide most LC disputes.

**In detail:** Art. 3 credits are irrevocable. Art. 4 the credit is independent of the sale contract. Art. 5 banks deal in documents, not goods. Art. 8 confirming bank. Art. 14 examination (max 5 banking days; presentation within 21 days of shipment). Art. 16 refusal. Art. 17 originals and copies. Art. 18 commercial invoice. Arts. 19-25 transport documents (20 bill of lading, 21 sea waybill, 22 charter-party B/L). Art. 27 clean transport document. Art. 28 insurance. Art. 30 tolerances. Art. 38 transferable. Art. 39 assignment of proceeds.

**Governing rules:** ICC UCP 600

**Related:** Documentary letter of credit (LC / DLC) - overview

### MT799 free-format messages
`bank-mt799` | sector: Payments and banking | stages: 3 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A bank-to-bank note with no payment obligation.

**In detail:** Banks use it to exchange information. It is widely abused in fraud, e.g. 'proof of funds' or 'pre-advice' messages sold by intermediaries. Genuine ones come bank to bank and create no obligation to pay.

**Risks and red flags:**
- Sold by intermediaries as 'proof of funds'
- Any request to pay a fee for one

### Common LC discrepancies and what to do

### ICPO (irrevocable corporate purchase order)
`doc-icpo` | sector: Commercial documents | stages: 1 | level: foundation | confidence: high

**In plain words:** A buyer's statement that it will buy on the terms of an offer.

**In detail:** Market custom in broker chains, not defined by law. The word 'irrevocable' does not make it binding by itself; effect depends on wording and law. - Unverifiable sender

## Processes, checklists and chain catalogue

### Catalogue of intermediary chains
`proc-chain-catalogue` | sector: Compliance, chains and fraud | stages: 0 | level: core | confidence: high

**In plain words:** The usual shapes a deal chain can take, from safest to most dangerous.

**In detail:** A deal chain runs from the original seller to the final buyer. On the seller side the safest shape is a producer or collector selling directly to the plant. Next comes a chain of title, where a collector sells to a trader who sells to the plant: each party buys and resells, so the certificates must be continuous. A broker standing between two principals is your target position and is low to moderate risk when you hold a written commission agreement. A mandate holder presenting the seller's offer is moderate risk and needs the mandate verified. A mandate holder who passes the mandate on to another mandate holder is high risk, because the first mandate must allow it. Several brokers in series, with no mandate, are high risk: commissions stack and nobody has authority. Introducers who only know someone are very high risk. On the buyer side the safest shape is the plant's own purchasing employee, then a group trading company buying for the group's plants, then a buying agent with a written mandate. A trader buying to resell is a real principal but depends on finding its own buyer, so its payment capacity needs proof. Chains of brokers or mandates on the buyer side are high risk, and a buyer with no plant, no offtake and no bank behind it is a red flag. Three special patterns cut across both sides: a string of principals selling the same cargo down the line, a circular trade, and a phantom principal.

**Options and variants:**
- Direct sale: Producer or collector sells to the plant: lowest risk
- Chain of title: Collector sells to trader, trader sells to plant: moderate, certificates must be continuous
- Broker between two principals: Your target position: low to moderate with a written commission agreement
- Mandate holder presents the offer: Moderate: verify the mandate with the issuer
- Mandate passed to another mandate holder: High: the first mandate must allow it
- Brokers in series: High: commissions stack and nobody has authority
- Introducer chain: Very high: no authority to verify
- Plant purchasing employee: Lowest risk on the buyer side: confirm signing authority
- Group trading company buying for plants: Low to moderate: check who pays and who receives
- Buying agent with a mandate: Moderate: verify the mandate
- Trader buying to resell: Moderate to high: ask for proof of a final buyer and of payment capacity
- Chain of brokers on the buyer side: High: the real buyer is hidden
- Buyer with no plant or offtake: Red flag: cannot show funds or a bank
- String of principals on one cargo: The same cargo sold down the line, each holding title for a moment: moderate to high
- Circular trade: The flow returns to the starting party, with no real end buyer: red flag
- Phantom principal: A party posing as seller that has nothing: high risk
- Dual agency: One intermediary acting for both sides without saying so: high risk

**Related:** Who is who in a deal: principal, mandate holder, broker, introducer, employee; How to map a chain; Circular trades

### Feedstock glossary: UCO, POME oil, PFAD and relatives

### Where the money goes: cost stack, netback and your edge
`mkt-margin-logic` | sector: Market and pricing | stages: 1, 2 | level: core | confidence: medium

**In plain words:** Start from what the buyer will pay, subtract every cost, and see what the seller can realistically ask and where your fee fits.

**In detail:** Use the netback method. First take the buyer's target price: a named benchmark plus or minus a premium or discount, on a CIF or FOB basis. Then subtract, in order: ocean freight, cargo insurance (110% of value under CIF), inspection and sampling at load and discharge, terminal handling and heating, export clearance, inland transport to the port, pretreatment and quality adjustments, financing cost (LC fees plus interest for the days cash is tied up), certification premium, and every intermediary's commission. What is left is the most the seller can be paid at source. Compare it with the seller's real ask and with Malaysian local reference prices (MPOB planned an official UCO reference based on local delivered prices). An ask far below that netback points to bait pricing or hidden fraud; an ask far above means the buyer's price will not work. Every extra layer in the chain takes a fee from the same pool, so shorter chains leave room for a better price at both ends. Malaysia-specific levers: no export duty on UCO or POME oil as of an October 2025 report (duties were being considered); Indonesia's ban on crude POME and UCO exports pushes buyers toward other origins; MPOB licensing and monthly declarations give you documents to verify; and ports such as Port Klang and Pasir Gudang are the loading options to check for terminal capacity and heating. Your edge, to confirm and adjust: being physically in Kuala Lumpur lets you verify what most brokers only claim (site visits, terminal confirmations, direct contact with licensed collectors), which shortens chains and makes your introductions credible to European buyers. In the app, use the cost-stack calculator with your own quotes.

**Options and variants:**
- Buyer target price: Benchmark plus or minus premium, with basis and pricing period
- Ocean freight: Quote for the exact route, vessel size and date
- Cargo insurance: 110% of cargo value under CIF
- Inspection and sampling: Load port and discharge port
- Terminal, heating and handling: Port and tank costs
- Export clearance and inland transport: Customs, licences, trucking to port
- Pretreatment and quality adjustment: Filtering, dehydration, penalties
- Financing cost: LC fees plus interest for days of cash tied up
- Certification premium: Cost of keeping the certified chain
- Intermediary commissions: Every layer takes a share of the same pool

**Related:** Catalogue of intermediary chains; Price benchmarks; LC costs and who pays

**Verification note:** The 'edge' paragraph is an assumption about your position; adjust it. Cost figures must come from real quotes.


## Scenarios

### The intermediary who wants an NCNDA but no video call
*Stage 0 | Compliance, chains and fraud | foundation*

**Situation:** A contact offers 5,000 tonnes a month of UCO. He insists you sign his NCNDA first but refuses a video call and will not send registry documents.

**Question:** What do you do?
- Sign the NCNDA to show good faith and ask for documents afterwards
- (correct) Refuse to proceed until the company and its decision-makers can be verified
- Pass the offer to your buyer and let the buyer check
- Ask for a small fee-free sample first

**Why:** A chain that cannot show who stands at either end has no value. Signing first gives away your protection and your contacts, and passing it to the buyer risks your credibility.

**What to do:**
- Ask for registry extract, directors and beneficial owners
- Insist on a video call with a decision-maker
- Verify certificates in public databases
- Walk away if refused

### 5,000 tonnes a month from one supplier
*Stage 0 | Compliance, chains and fraud | core*

**Situation:** A supplier claims it can ship 5,000 tonnes of UCO every month from Malaysia. It sends an ISCC certificate and a price well below the market benchmark.

**Question:** What is the first sensible check?
- Accept the price because it is below market
- (correct) Check the certificate in the public database and test whether the collection network could really generate that volume
- Ask the buyer to prepay a deposit
- Ask for a larger discount

**Why:** Collecting that volume from restaurants and food plants needs a very large network. A price far below benchmark, with volume beyond plausible capacity, is a classic sign of virgin oil being relabelled.

**What to do:**
- Verify certificate scope, sites and validity
- Ask how many points of origin and which collectors
- Ask for MPOB licence numbers and monthly declarations
- Visit the site or have an independent party visit

### Four brokers between you and the seller
*Stage 0 | Compliance, chains and fraud | core*

**Situation:** Your contact says the seller is 'behind three other brokers' but will pay everyone a share.

**Question:** What does this tell you?
- It is normal and safe if everyone signs an NCNDA
- (correct) Commissions will stack, nobody may have authority, and the price will probably not work
- The seller must be very large
- You should demand a higher share

**Why:** Each layer takes a fee from the same pool, and authority rarely reaches the owner. More than two undocumented layers is a stop.

**What to do:**
- Ask who the principal is and who signs the sales contract
- Ask for the mandate documents of each layer
- Try to reach the principal directly
- Walk away if layers cannot be documented

### A sister company contacts your buyer
*Stage 0 | Compliance, chains and fraud | advanced*

**Situation:** Weeks after you introduced a plant to a seller, a different company with the same address contacts the plant directly with a similar offer.

**Question:** What should your paperwork have covered?
- Nothing; this is normal
- (correct) Parties defined to include affiliates, successors and beneficial owners
- A larger fee
- A shorter contract

**Why:** Defining the parties to include affiliates and beneficial owners closes the 'second company' route that is used to bypass brokers.

**What to do:**
- Check beneficial owners
- Include affiliates in the NCNDA and commission agreement
- Document your introduction in writing

### An ICPO that does not mention price
*Stage 1 | Commercial documents | foundation*

**Situation:** A buyer's ICPO lists product, volume, port and payment method but no price or discount.

**Question:** What do you do?
- Start shopping it to sellers anyway
- (correct) Ask for the price basis: benchmark, premium or discount, basis and pricing period
- Assume the market price
- Ask the seller to propose the price

**Why:** Without a price basis you negotiate blind against the market. The buyer's target price gives you the ceiling and lets you test every offer.

**What to do:**
- Request the target price formula
- Confirm FOB or CIF basis
- Confirm the pricing period

### Proof of product that nobody can verify
*Stage 1 | Commercial documents | core*

**Situation:** A seller sends pixelated PDFs and a generic video. It refuses to let anyone contact the terminal or visit.

**Question:** How do you treat this?
- As sufficient, because it sent documents
- (correct) As a strong warning sign and ask for terminal confirmation or a visit
- As proof of title
- Ask the buyer to accept it

**Why:** Refusal of independent verification is a strong red flag. Copies prove nothing about existence, custody or title.

**What to do:**
- Ask the terminal to confirm in writing
- Ask for upstream invoices and inspector reports
- Arrange a visit or independent sampling

### Pay a fee to see a sample
*Stage 1 | Compliance, chains and fraud | foundation*

**Situation:** A seller says a sample will be released after you pay a 'registration and handling' fee.

**Question:** What is this?
- A normal cost of doing business
- (correct) The classic advance-fee pattern
- A sign of a premium supplier
- A discount for the buyer

**Why:** Fees for registration, proof, inspection or bonds before a verified contract are the advance-fee pattern.

**What to do:**
- Decline
- Ask for independent verification instead
- Report if you were asked to introduce a buyer

### A large buyer will not sign your NCNDA
*Stage 1 | Commercial documents | core*

**Situation:** The buyer's compliance team says it never signs third-party NCNDAs but is happy to work with you.

**Question:** What is the best protection?
- Walk away
- (correct) A direct commission agreement with the company, signed by an authorised signatory
- Rely on a verbal promise
- Ask the seller to pay you more

**Why:** Large companies often refuse third-party NCNDAs. A commission agreement with the buyer itself is the most solid protection, and it should name affiliates and the payout trigger.

**What to do:**
- Ask who is authorised to sign
- Define payer, trigger, route, duration and roll-overs
- Keep copies of every introduction

### English law or Spanish law?
*Stage 2 | Contracts and Incoterms | core*

**Situation:** The seller's draft says English law with London arbitration. The Spanish buyer wants Spanish law.

**Question:** What is the right way to think about it?
- English law is mandatory in commodity trading
- (correct) It is a choice: pick law and seat that both can accept, with an arbitration clause that is enforceable
- The seller always wins
- Governing law does not matter

**Why:** English law is common but not mandatory. Alternatives include Singapore, Malaysian or Spanish law with an agreed institution. Enforceability under the New York Convention matters.

**What to do:**
- Compare institutions and seats
- Match the contract and the standard forms used
- Take legal advice

### A contract that says nothing about sustainability
*Stage 2 | Regulation and sustainability | core*

**Situation:** The draft SPA covers price, quantity and delivery but is silent on certificates and proof of sustainability.

**Question:** What is missing?
- Nothing; certificates are assumed
- (correct) Warranties about certification, a proof of sustainability with each cargo, mass balance duties and remedies if certificates fail
- A longer arbitration clause
- A higher price

**Why:** The product is worth what the regulation lets it do. If the certificates fail, the buyer loses value, so the contract must make them a condition.

**What to do:**
- Add sustainability compliance clause
- Make documents a payment condition
- Add indemnity and termination rights

### Crude POME from Indonesia
*Stage 2 | Regulation and sustainability | core*

**Situation:** A seller offers crude POME oil loading in Indonesia.

**Question:** What must you check first?
- The price
- (correct) Whether crude POME and crude UCO exports are currently banned there and what grade is actually offered
- The vessel
- The payment currency

**Why:** Indonesia has banned exports of crude POME and crude UCO since January 2025 (confirmed as maintained in February 2026). Refined grades were still exported. Always check the current rules.

**What to do:**
- Check the current rule and the product grade
- Ask for export permits
- Include a change-in-law clause

### Escrow for a full cargo
*Stage 3 | Payments and banking | core*

**Situation:** A buyer proposes to pay a 5,000 tonne cargo through a law firm's escrow account.

**Question:** Why is this unusual?
- It is the standard for bulk cargoes
- (correct) Funds are locked while release depends on a third party, and a waiting ship runs up demurrage; letters of credit are the norm
- It is cheaper
- It avoids all documents

**Why:** Escrow is rarely used for bulk cargoes. A confirmed LC or a standby-backed arrangement fits the physical timeline better.

**What to do:**
- Ask why not a letter of credit
- Check the escrow agent independently
- Consider escrow only for small parcels

### Buy an SBLC from a provider
*Stage 3 | Payments and banking | advanced*

**Situation:** An intermediary says you can 'lease' or 'monetize' an SBLC for a fee to guarantee the deal.

**Question:** What is it?
- A normal bank service
- (correct) A known scam pattern: banks issue instruments to their own clients, not as tradeable products
- A way to lower cost
- A requirement of the ICC

**Why:** Instruments are not freely traded products. Real bank discounting of a genuine LC is a different thing.

**What to do:**
- Decline
- Verify any instrument bank to bank
- Use your own bank relationships

### A letter of credit by email
*Stage 3 | Payments and banking | core*

**Situation:** A broker forwards a PDF 'LC' from the buyer's bank.

**Question:** How do you check?
- Trust it because it has a logo
- (correct) Have the seller's bank confirm it received the SWIFT message from the issuing bank
- Ask the broker to confirm
- Ask for another PDF

**Why:** A real LC is sent bank to bank by SWIFT. A PDF is not an LC.

**What to do:**
- Ask the seller's bank to verify the SWIFT message
- Never act on the PDF alone

### The LC does not match the contract
*Stage 3 | Payments and banking | core*

**Situation:** The LC names a different discharge port and a quantity tolerance narrower than the SPA.

**Question:** What now?
- Load anyway and fix it later
- (correct) Ask the buyer to amend the LC before loading
- Ignore the difference
- Ask the bank to waive it

**Why:** After loading, mismatches cannot be repaired without risk. Amendments must be done before loading.

**What to do:**
- Compare line by line
- Ask for an amendment
- Do not load until fixed

### The vessel that does not exist
*Stage 4 | Shipping, inspection and customs | core*

**Situation:** The seller nominates a vessel whose IMO number belongs to a ship scrapped months ago.

**Question:** What do you do?
- Accept; names change
- (correct) Treat it as a serious red flag and verify the vessel and its owners
- Ask for a discount
- Proceed to loading

**Why:** A ghost vessel is a known pattern. Verify the IMO number, history, owner and sanctions status in public databases.

**What to do:**
- Check public ship databases
- Screen owner and manager
- Request the nomination again

### Two inspectors, two answers
*Stage 4 | Shipping, inspection and customs | advanced*

**Situation:** The load-port inspector reports the cargo within spec; the buyer's laboratory says it is out of spec.

**Question:** What decides?
- Whichever is worse
- (correct) The contract: named inspector, final-and-binding rule, sealed samples and any umpire procedure
- The ship's captain
- The broker

**Why:** Disputes are settled by the contract's procedure, using sealed samples and, if agreed, an umpire laboratory.

**What to do:**
- Preserve the sealed samples
- Follow the notice period
- Escalate per the clause

### The ship arrives before the original bill of lading
*Stage 5 | Shipping, inspection and customs | core*

**Situation:** The vessel is at the discharge port but the original B/L is still travelling through banks.

**Question:** What is the safe route?
- Release cargo without paperwork
- (correct) A letter of indemnity in the form agreed in the contract, normally backed by a bank
- Wait indefinitely
- Ask the broker to sign

**Why:** The B/L is the title document. An indemnity lets the carrier release the cargo, but it should be pre-agreed and supported.

**What to do:**
- Check the contract clause
- Get the indemnity countersigned by a bank
- Keep records

### A switch bill of lading
*Stage 5 | Shipping, inspection and customs | advanced*

**Situation:** A trader proposes to replace the original B/L with a new one in its own name to hide the supplier.

**Question:** What is the issue?
- None
- (correct) It can obscure origin and traceability, which clashes with chain-of-custody rules, and is used in fraud
- It is required
- It lowers freight

**Why:** Switch bills can break the traceability that certified sustainable material needs. Do not accept without understanding why.

**What to do:**
- Ask for the reason
- Check certification continuity
- Escalate to compliance

### Relabel UCO as SPO to fit a licence
*Stage 5 | Regulation and sustainability | advanced*

**Situation:** A Malaysian exporter suggests declaring UCO as sludge palm oil because its licence only covers SPO.

**Question:** What do you do?
- Accept to save time
- (correct) Refuse and check what the product really is under current MPOB and customs rules
- Ask the buyer to pay for a licence
- Declare half under each

**Why:** Declare exactly what the product is. Misdeclaring goods can lead to penalties and seizure and is the type of fraud authorities are now targeting.

**What to do:**
- Ask for MPOB licence and customs export licence
- Seek legal advice on classification
- Do not proceed with a misdeclared cargo

### A buyer asks for EUDR guarantees
*Stage 2 | Regulation and sustainability | foundation*

**Situation:** A European buyer asks for EUDR statements for a UCO cargo.

**Question:** What do you do?
- Refuse to discuss
- (correct) Check whether the product is in scope or exempt waste or residue, and use the right application dates
- Ignore it
- Agree to anything

**Why:** The EUDR applies from 30 December 2026 for large and medium operators, and scope for waste or residues depends on classification and the delegated act. Ask the buyer for its position.

**What to do:**
- Ask for the buyer's legal position
- Document classification
- Add a regulatory clause

### The commission 'will be paid later'
*Stage 6 | Payments and banking | core*

**Situation:** The seller promises your fee after the deal but refuses to put payout terms in writing.

**Question:** What do you do?
- Trust
- (correct) Do not proceed without a written commission agreement with payer, trigger, route and roll-overs
- Ask for double
- Ask the buyer to pay instead

**Why:** Without a written payout trigger and route, your fee depends on goodwill. Banks do not pay it automatically from the SPA.

**What to do:**
- Draft a commission agreement
- Define payer, trigger and wire route
- Consider an assignment of proceeds if the bank agrees
